# Team respect and success: research for Overwatch Esports at IU Indianapolis

Research date: November 16, 2026. This is a research and design recommendation, not an approved policy or a legal assessment. It supplements the current [player code of conduct](player-code-of-conduct.md). Examples below are proposed club practices unless attributed to a source.

## Recommendation

Build the framework around a positive promise: members help one another compete, learn, and belong while respecting each person's dignity and boundaries. Explain what teammates and leaders do to make that promise real. Keep clear conduct boundaries and fair reporting procedures alongside it.

The current code already welcomes good-faith criticism, distinguishes frustration from mistreatment, protects reporting, and separates coaching conversations from disciplinary findings. Its main opportunity is to describe everyday successful teamwork with comparable specificity. Players need to know how to prepare, disagree about a composition, respond to a mistake, ask for a break, and recover after a bad scrim. Coaches and officers need equally explicit responsibilities.

Use three connected documents or clearly separated parts: a club-wide code; a short team agreement completed by each roster; and a practical guide for feedback, debriefs, and repair. Cross-reference the existing accountability procedures rather than creating competing disciplinary systems. A roster agreement must not create new disciplinary authority or override the club's approved protections.

## What the evidence supports

### Psychological safety supports learning, with limits on causal claims

Amy Edmondson's [1999 study of 51 manufacturing work teams](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Group_Performance/Edmondson%20Psychological%20safety.pdf) found an association between psychological safety and learning behavior. The paper treats asking for help, discussing errors, seeking feedback, and experimenting as learning behaviors. This is empirical field research, not a trial showing that a written conduct code causes better results.

For this club, the useful inference is to make it ordinary to say "I lost track of their ultimate," "I don't understand the rotation," or "I disagree with that read." Leaders should acknowledge their own errors and examine what information players had. Psychological safety should support demanding review and honest disagreement. It should not become an expectation that every interaction feels comfortable or that performance decisions disappear.

Google's [Project Aristotle account](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness) identifies psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact as relevant team dynamics. This is a primary organizational research account combined with practitioner guidance, not an independently replicated esports experiment. Its useful contribution is to pair interpersonal trust with reliable commitments and clear roles. It does not establish that individual skill is unimportant in Overwatch.

### Agreements work best alongside a plan for the actual task

[Mathieu and Rapp's 2009 study](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013257) examined 32 MBA student teams in a business simulation. The authors report that teams combining high-quality charters with high-quality performance strategies had the highest sustained performance. This supports attending to both how people work together and how they will accomplish their task. It does not establish that signing a charter alone improves team performance.

The direct club application is to pair a team agreement with a practice plan. "Respect each other" needs operational companions: who schedules scrims, how substitutes receive preparation, who makes the final live call, and which skill the next block will train. A highly respectful roster can still struggle if nobody owns those tasks.

### Debriefing has broader empirical support

[Tannenbaum and Cerasoli's 2013 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812448394) combined 46 samples involving 2,136 participants and found an average advantage for debriefing over controls. [Keiser and Arthur's later meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000821), published online in 2020 and in a 2021 issue, examined 61 studies and found benefits that depended on combinations of review characteristics, including alignment with the team or individual and objective review media.

These are research syntheses, not primary experiments in collegiate Overwatch. They justify trying focused reviews that connect evidence to an actionable next attempt. They do not justify promising a particular increase in wins. Replay timestamps are a practical candidate for objective review material; the exact routine proposed below remains an adaptation to test locally.

## Concrete prior art worth adapting

### Esports codes state affirmative player and coach duties

The [British Esports Student Champs player code](https://champs.britishesports.org/conduct) asks players to encourage teammates, recognize different skill levels, set a positive example, and practice sportsmanship in victory and defeat. These are observable contributions to a team, alongside prohibitions. Its [coaching code page](https://britishesports.org/esports-coaching/coaching-code-of-conduct/) places responsibilities on leaders to protect welfare, maintain boundaries, and model appropriate behavior.

These are governing-body standards and safeguarding advice, not evidence that a particular phrase changes behavior. Their school and college context includes youth-specific arrangements. Adapt the affirmative commitments to a university club; do not import parental involvement, eligibility restrictions, or disciplinary procedures wholesale.

For example, a club commitment could ask returning players to explain established comms and scrim routines to newcomers. Coaches could commit to explaining development goals and providing a predictable opportunity to discuss feedback. Both make support reciprocal rather than equating respect with obedience.

### Sports codes make coach responsibilities explicit

USA Swimming publishes separate model codes for [athletes](https://www.usaswimming.org/docs/default-source/diversity-inclusion/resource-guides/model-team-code-of-conduct-athletes.pdf) and [coaches](https://www.usaswimming.org/docs/default-source/diversity-inclusion/resource-guides/model-team-code-of-conduct-coach.pdf). Athletes commit to sportsmanship, attendance, courtesy, and respecting personal space. Coaches commit to positive instruction, fair treatment, professional boundaries, and prioritizing athlete welfare over performance.

The transferable design is reciprocal obligation. Players should prepare and communicate availability; coaches should prepare sessions and communicate changes. Competitive status should not excuse mistreatment by either. These are normative examples, not outcome studies. Avoid copying broad restrictions on offensive language or youth attendance rules when the club's own policy uses more specific conduct standards.

### University group agreements turn values into decisions

The University of Waterloo's [Making Group Contracts guide](https://uwaterloo.ca/centre-for-teaching-excellence/catalogs/tip-sheets/making-group-contracts) recommends a small set of essential expectations covering preparation, communication, responsibilities, unmet expectations, and ongoing feedback. Its sample includes seeking help before a missed deadline disrupts the group. This is teaching guidance with a usable example, not a controlled evaluation of that template.

A roster can adapt that approach to agree on response times, scrim readiness, absence notices, and review participation. Members should discuss these expectations together. Officers should identify which club rules are fixed before that discussion so players do not mistake local agreement for permission to waive protections.

## Recommended framework structure

### 1. Shared purpose and reciprocal commitments

Open with the club's intended experience: competitive improvement, enjoyment, belonging, and sustainable participation alongside university life. Translate that purpose into a few commitments applicable to players, substitutes, coaches, and officers:

- Help teammates learn by sharing useful information and recognizing specific improvements.
- Prepare for agreed activities, communicate changes early, and ask for help when commitments become unrealistic.
- Challenge decisions with evidence and curiosity; describe actions without attacking a person's worth.
- Respect boundaries and allow people to raise concerns without earning a reputation as difficult.
- Acknowledge mistakes, make practical corrections, and follow through on agreed repair.

Keep enforceable boundaries clear. An aspiration such as encouraging teammates should not silently become a disciplinary rule requiring enthusiasm, extroversion, friendship, or constant positivity. Quiet participation and respectful disagreement can contribute to a successful team.

### 2. A roster agreement and practice plan

At the start of a semester, give each roster a facilitated discussion and one-page template. Include substitutes and coaches. Ask what level of competition and time commitment members can sustain, then record:

- Practice times, readiness expectations, absence notification, and who arranges substitutes.
- Scheduling channels and a realistic response window outside matches, with no expectation of constant availability.
- Live decision roles, how to flag missing information, and when strategic disagreements return for review.
- How players request feedback, raise workload concerns, or ask to revisit an agreement.
- A small number of current practice goals and a date to review progress.

Coaches should explain selection criteria and the route for asking about a roster decision. Keep performance selection distinct from misconduct findings. Concerns about attendance should begin by clarifying facts and constraints rather than assuming disrespect. Apply the club's established procedures whenever a response would restrict participation.

### 3. Feedback that produces a next action

The Center for Creative Leadership's [Situation-Behavior-Impact guidance](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/) recommends describing a specific event, observable behavior, and its impact, then asking about intent. Treat this as a practitioner tool. The page alone does not establish its effectiveness in this club.

Adapt it with a proposed next action: "On our second defense, you pushed before the regroup call. We entered separately and lost our next setup. What information were you acting on? Can we confirm five before the next engage?" The recipient can correct the factual account or explain that the call was inaudible. Agree on an adjustment after hearing that information.

Apply the same specificity to praise: "You called the enemy flank early, so both supports rotated safely." Avoid global labels such as "uncoachable" or "always feeding." In live play, keep feedback short and actionable. Save contested explanations for review. Agreement to receive coaching does not require tolerating personal insults.

## Routine practices to try

The following timings are practical starting points, not research-established doses.

Before each block, spend two minutes confirming availability, decision roles, and one learning goal. Allow a player to say they need a lighter session without disclosing private medical or personal details to the roster.

During play, use a brief reset phrase such as "next fight, regroup first." Any member may flag that comms have become personal or unusable. At an appropriate break, the designated leader checks what needs to change. Respect tournament pause rules; a reset cue is not permission to abandon a live match. Repeatedly using the cue to silence tactical disagreement should itself prompt review.

After a block, hold a five- to ten-minute debrief. Ask what the team intended, what happened, what helped, and what one adjustment it will test next time. Use one or two replay moments instead of a catalogue of individual failures. Give everyone an opportunity to contribute, including in writing afterward. Record the next action and its owner, not a public ranking of blame.

Every few weeks, revisit the agreement. Ask whether people can request help, whether commitments are realistic, and whether coaching produces useful next steps. In a small roster, anonymous comments can still be identifiable. Explain privacy limits and offer a separate officer contact. Do not treat survey answers as disciplinary evidence or make win rate the sole measure of success.

## Conflict repair with safeguards

The Restorative Justice Council's [principles](https://restorativejustice.org.uk/sites/default/files/Restorative%20Principles.pdf) emphasize voluntary participation, acknowledgment of impact, participant needs, and safety. Its [organizational code](https://restorativejustice.org.uk/restorative-organisation-code-practice) requires informed consent that can be withdrawn, appropriate safeguards, and practitioner preparation. These are professional practice standards, not proof that an informal captain-led conversation will resolve harm.

For ordinary friction, an officer could offer a voluntary supported conversation after checking separately with participants. Ask what happened, how participation was affected, what each person needs, and what concrete change is feasible. A repair might involve correcting a misleading public statement, changing comms behavior, or agreeing on a check-in date. Do not require public apologies, forgiveness, friendship, or a face-to-face meeting.

Preserve the current code's direct reporting route. Nobody should have to confront someone or attempt mediation first. For threats, harassment, retaliation, coercion, or major power imbalances, officers should consult the advisor and appropriate university channels rather than improvise restorative facilitation. Repair may accompany appropriate accountability; it must not suppress a report or replace required procedures. A coach who controls selection should not facilitate a complaint about their own behavior.

## Limits and implementation priorities

This review found transferable evidence and concrete institutional examples, but no direct evaluation of this proposed framework in a collegiate Overwatch club. Manufacturing teams, workplace teams, student simulations, and training studies differ from a volunteer esports roster. The charter and debrief meta-analysis claims above were verified against author abstracts through Europe PMC; their paywalled full methods were not reviewed. Edmondson's full paper and the institutional guidance were read directly. Source selection was targeted rather than systematic.

Start with one roster agreement workshop, a brief leader orientation on feedback and reporting, and a regular debrief. Review after four to six weeks with players, substitutes, coaches, and the advisor. Ask whether members can speak up, understand decisions, meet realistic commitments, and see follow-through. More reports may reflect greater trust, so report counts alone should not define failure. Use the existing consultation and approval process before adopting substantive policy changes. This report leaves the current policy unchanged and makes no conclusions about legal compliance.
